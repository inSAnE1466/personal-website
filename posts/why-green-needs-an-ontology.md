---
title: Why Green needs an ontology
description: How evidence about technologies, sites and resources can support a decision you can check.
draft: true
---
There is an extraordinary amount of useful public information right now: technical work, standards, government and patent databases, supply chain and manufacturing information, datasets, open-source software. The failure I want to avoid is letting it disappear into chat histories, or copying it into folders nobody reads again.

A pile of documents does not help someone decide whether a technology belongs in their building. What helps is being able to ask a narrow question and get back the smallest set of evidence that answers it, with a clear account of where that evidence came from.

That is what I mean by an ontology. The information is organized around real things: technologies, sites, companies, resources, suppliers, regulations, and the links between them. A claim stays connected to its source. Contradictions and uncertainty stay visible instead of being smoothed over.

I want the ontology to serve as the index too. Building it should be the same work as making the evidence searchable: finding a technology, what it links to, where it has been used and what was decided about it. If that works, it should compound, because each new object has more to connect to.

For Green, that could look something like this. A technology has what it does, what it needs and what it has been used for. A site has what it runs today and what it draws on. A decision is the link between them, and it carries the evidence behind it: which sources, which past cases, which assumptions, and what is still missing.

A person reviewing that decision should be able to open each piece. If a number was calculated, they should see its inputs and method. If a question could not be answered from the evidence, it should show up as a gap, not a guess.

I think the jargon and the scientific connections are the hard part. Some of it may be solved in the application layer. Some may need a small model, fine-tuned on secondary and proprietary data, to classify what belongs where. That needs more thinking, and it is one of the questions I most want to test.

General agent capabilities will get easier to reproduce. A deep, current, usable understanding of a field is harder to reproduce, especially when it is connected to workflows that have been tested and to real outcomes. That is why I think the ontology, more than any single feature, is where the lasting value in a vertical sits.
